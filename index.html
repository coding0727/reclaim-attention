My story

Why I started this

There was one evening that genuinely made me stop and think. After work, I wanted to relax by watching a TV show. It should have been simple: turn on the television, choose something interesting, and press play. Instead, almost without thinking, I opened Instagram.

That moment surprised me. Not because I preferred social media, but because opening the app felt automatic. I realized I had stopped making conscious choices about how I spent my free time.

I've used social media for nearly a decade. For many years, I was mindful of my screen time and didn't think much of it. But after starting a new job a few years ago, I began treating social media as a small reward after a long day. My work also involves frequent waiting periods — usually just three to ten minutes while tasks finish running. Those short breaks felt too brief to start anything meaningful, so I naturally reached for my phone.

"What do you enjoy doing after work?" I struggled to answer.

I wasn't learning something new. I wasn't working on creative projects. I wasn't spending much time on hobbies I used to enjoy. Where had all those evenings gone? I couldn't remember much of what I had watched online, but I could clearly feel that I had gradually lost control over how I was spending my time.

That realization became the beginning of my digital detox journey. I began experimenting with small changes — not because social media is inherently bad, but because I wanted my attention to reflect my values rather than my habits.

Over time, I discovered strategies that made a meaningful difference. This website is a collection of those experiences, along with evidence-informed resources that helped me better understand what was happening.

I'm not a therapist, psychologist, or medical professional. Everything shared here is intended for educational purposes and is based on my personal experience together with publicly available research. My hope is simply that something here helps you reclaim a little more of your time, attention, and peace of mind.

Why We Scroll — and What It Costs

Before changing a habit, it helps to understand it — both what makes it so easy to fall into, and what it quietly takes from us in return.

Why is social media so hard to put down?

Modern social media platforms are intentionally designed to encourage engagement. Features such as infinite scrolling, personalized recommendations, notifications, and variable rewards make it easy to continue using an app longer than originally intended.

None of these features automatically make social media harmful. Many people use social media in healthy and positive ways. However, if scrolling begins replacing activities that matter to you — or if you regularly finish using social media feeling mentally drained — it may be worth taking a closer look at your habits.

Attention is a limited resource

Our brains constantly decide what deserves attention. When we spend long periods rapidly switching between hundreds of unrelated pieces of information, our attention is repeatedly redirected. Research suggests that frequent interruptions and constant task-switching can make sustained concentration more difficult and leave people feeling mentally fatigued.

Personally, I noticed this at work. Meetings felt harder to sit through without checking email or thinking about something else. I constantly wanted more stimulation, even when the meeting was important. That was one of the biggest warning signs for me.

Why scrolling doesn't always feel relaxing

Many of us scroll because we're tired. Ironically, after long scrolling sessions, we often don't feel truly rested — passive consumption doesn't always provide the kind of recovery our minds need.

For me, scrolling often left me feeling mentally full but emotionally unsatisfied. I had consumed hundreds of pieces of information, yet very little felt meaningful or memorable.

Social comparison

Social media allows us to learn, connect, and stay informed. It can also encourage constant comparison with carefully curated versions of other people's lives. Research has linked heavy social media use with poorer mental well-being for some individuals, although the relationship is complex and varies from person to person.

Being intentional about what we follow — and how much time we spend scrolling — can help create a healthier experience.

Start a Detox

A digital detox doesn't require deleting every app forever — just enough space to choose your attention instead of acting on autopilot.

Why it matters

Why a digital detox matters

For me, reducing social media helped me feel more present, rediscover hobbies, spend more uninterrupted time reading, and feel less mentally cluttered at the end of the day.

Everyone's experience will be different. The goal isn't perfection — it's intentionality.

Three places to begin

Three ways to reduce the pull

Reduce stimulation

  • Turn your phone to grayscale
  • Keep your phone on silent
  • Turn off non-essential notifications
  • Remove notification badges

Reduce temptation

  • Set app time limits
  • Move apps off your home screen
  • Keep your phone in another room in the evening
  • Create phone-free moments at meals or before bed

Reduce algorithm influence

  • Turn off personalized recommendations where possible
  • Pause watch history if appropriate
  • Subscribe intentionally instead of relying on endless recommendations

Replace the Habit

Removing a habit is difficult if nothing replaces it. Instead of asking "what should I stop doing," try asking "what would I like to do instead."

A gentle progression

Four levels, from gentlest to hardest

Small changes are often easier to sustain than dramatic ones. These are arranged from the gentlest starting point to the most challenging — there's no need to start at Level 1 if you're already further along.

01 · gentle transitions

Start low-stimulation

If putting your phone away feels difficult, start with lower-stimulation activities on your phone.

  • Sudoku
  • Puzzle games
  • Word games
  • Language learning apps
02 · hands-on hobbies

Engage your hands

When you're ready to spend less time on your phone, these provide gentle engagement without endless novelty.

  • Coloring
  • LEGO
  • Sticker art
  • Journaling
  • Crafts
03 · movement

Reset your body

Sometimes the best reset is simply moving. Even one or two minutes can interrupt automatic scrolling.

  • Stretch
  • Walk around the room
  • Drink water
  • Step outside
  • Take a short walk
04 · stillness

Sit with quiet

The most challenging step for many. Can you be comfortable without constant stimulation?

  • Close your eyes for one minute
  • Practice slow breathing
  • Meditate
  • Simply sit quietly

Learning to be alone with our thoughts is a skill — and like any skill, it improves with practice.
